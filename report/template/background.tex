\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
In this section we provide an overview of background literature in current academic field.. Through this, we aim to embed our project in this field, and present the foundations of our research. We start by outlining theories on human movement in physical and virtual space, and explain our intuitions for extending this to information space, after this we describe conceptualisations of movement and distances in virtual space, specifically focussing on Wikipedia. Next we conceptualise distance in targeted search, finally we briefly describe the position of the current paper in the described niche. 

\subsection{Navigating Physical/Virtual space}
\subsubsection{Signal-to-noise ratio}
Shannon's Theorem describes the information capacity $C$ (in bits/s) of a communications channel of bandwidth $B$ as: \[C=B\log_2(SN+1)\]
Fitts proposed an index of difficulty (ID) for a target acquisition task where signal power (S) and noise power (N) are replaced by movement amplitude (A) and target width (W), respectively. This was later reformulated to the Shannon notation,as:

\[ID=\log_2(\frac{A}{W}+1)\]

According to MacKenzie\say{In Fitts' analogy, movement amplitudes are like signals and target tolerances or widths are like noise.\cite{mackenzie2018fitts}}.

\subsubsection{Two phase movement}
In a 1996 paper Graham and MacKenzie\cite{physicalvirtualpointing} describe a finding by Woodworth\cite{pointing} that human pointing movement consists of two phases: \say{an initial planned impulse which covers most of the distance, followed by a second phase of deceleration to the target under current control}. They find evidence of similar behaviour in virtual pointing behaviour. Virtual pointing is conceptualized as mouse-movement towards a goal, which follows Fitts' law. 

\subsection{Navigating Information space}
In this paper we investigate navigation in information space, specifically targeted navigation from some starting-page to a goal-page. Specifically we are interested in investigating whether movement in this information space is similar to movement in physical space. One thing that should be noted is that the navigation in such information networks is restricted as it has to adhere to the graph strucutre, whereas the movement investigated by MacKenzie\cite{mackenzie2018fitts} is not. A motivation for our hypothesis that such an analogy is sensible is that the two problems seem similar, this is further supported by a similar two-phase movement as described by West et al. \cite{semdistances}. 

We conceputalize Shannon's Theorem in information space as follows: movement amplitudes could be computed over some distance over the two objects in the information space, the noise of an object would be the target tolerance akin to Fitts' law, which in networks would be the ease of access of a node (e.g. PageRank).


\subsection{How humans navigate Wikipedia}
To use this conceptualisation though, we need to define more concretely how we determine distances for targeted navigation on Wikipedia graphs. An obvious intuition would be to use the shortest path between nodes as the distance, however West et al. note that human behaviour differs from this\cite{humanwayfinding} in targeted Wikipedia navigation. They describe the aforementioned two-phase movement, where users initially move to a \say{hub}-page, which have a large number of outgoing edges to different topics, after this their movement is mostly guided by semantic features. West et al. later show that human search is very efficient, but uses vastly different navigation than shortest path, with semantic similarity explaining human navigation better\cite{humanwayfinding}. 

In targeted search we assume the subject is moving towards their goal as fast as they can. In physical space this mean they move the shortest path (on average), however as mentioned above shortest-path graph traversal is not observed in information space. Instead, navigation is guided by semantic similarity. However, humans still move according to a \textit{perceived} optimal way. Since navigation is guided by semantical similarity\cite{semdistances} a network with semantic distances, rather than graph distances might work bettter. 

\subsection{Semantic Distances}
There are several ways to define semantic distances. Popular baselines exist, such as WordNet or Word2Vec. These are zero-shot models which are known to provide semantic distances between words that are in line with human judgements. There are, however many different ways to find these distances. 

\subsubsection{Semantic distances from Wikipedia}
Several works investigate the possibility of extracting information on the semantic distance between concepts from Wikipedia\cite{efficientwordnetwikipedia, extendingwordnet, wikipediaconceptmaps}. They show that useful information can be extracted from the Wikipedia graph structure. The aforementioned papers mostly focus on information that can directly be extracted from the structure of data within Wikipedia. However, there are other aspects to consider as well, such as the way this Wikipedia data-structure is actually utilized by its users. West et al. \cite{semdistances} investigate the possibility of extracting semantic distances from a dataset of Wikispeedia games. This dataset consists of network traversal paths, where each path represents a person playing the Wikipedia game. In this game, a user is tasked to navigate from source-page A to goal-page B in the least amount of steps, thus these traces show the behaviour of targeted navigation on the Wikipedia network. The most relevant finding in this paper is that these traces can be analysed to determine semantic distances between concepts that are sensible when presented to human evaluators, improving over an LSA baseline. This approach was extended in 2013 by Singer et al.\cite{semdistancesbetter} to include distances for items which don't directly occur in the same path, and used a larger dataset. A 2016 paper \cite{semanticsunconstrainedwikipedia} furthermore finds that even traces from unconstrained navigation can be used to infer semantic similarity between topics.

\subsubsection{Semantic distances from transformers}
In a 2016 paper, Nogueira and Cho apply these concepts to create a model which produces random walks between nodes on the Wikipedia graph\cite{LSTMembedding}. They reference the work by West et al. stating that they \say{see that the goal-driven web navigation is a generalization and re-framing of the Wikispeedia}. Their approach places this concept of semantic navigation at its core, representing semantic similarity as a small distance between bag-of-words and attention mechanism embaddings.

In 2022 Zaheer et al. extend the approach outlined by Nogueira and Cho by using transformer models for embedding rather than a bag-of-words approach. Their method vastly improves baselines, at times approaching or improving over state-of-the-art methods. The paper by Zaheer et al. shows strong evidence that semantic similarity extracted by transformers facilitate a good navigation policy \say{Using random features in place of [transformers] caused the performance to drop to near chance, showing that the models are utilizing the semantics at each node and are not relying on a general search strategy of some kind}.

\subsection{This paper}
Inspired by the Shannon formulation of Fitts's law and the evidence for two-phase, semantically-guided movement in targeted Wikipedia navigation, this paper investigates an analogous relationship for the task difficulty and the number of clicks required to complete a search. We adopt the Fitts' Law analogy, but for the distance component we take into account the semantic distance between the source and goal pages. As human navigation does not follow optimal-graph navigation. The target tolerance is conceptualised as the page-rank. Rather than extracting distance from our data. We do this because otherwise there is a risk of data contamination in our correlation analysis. Zaheer et al.\cite{embeddingrandomwalks} use RoBERTa, we use a small BERT model. 

An analysis of user behaviour on Wikipedia is conducted by Piccardi et al. in 2023\cite{overviewwikipedianavigation}, and again by Piccardi and West in 2025 \cite{overviewwikipedianavigation2}. They find that Wikipedia usage does not always follow the same structure as targeted search, meaning results do not generalize to all Wikipedia usage. Specifically natural Wikipedia usage is often very short, only one (or very few) clicks. This means that the results in this paper only apply to targeted navigation on Wikipedia.
