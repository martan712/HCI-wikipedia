\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Browsing the internet has become an integral part of everyday modern life. The internet is often subdivided into connected information networks that humans navigate using their internal models of the world. Understanding how humans navigate through such information networks gives insight into the connectedness of topics from a user's viewpoint. With this knowledge of semantic relations, the curators of these networks can organise their network nodes in a way that allows for more efficient and intuitive navigation.

Wikipedia is a well-known example of a virtual information network that can be represented as a directed graph with articles as nodes and links between articles as the edges. It is partially structured by the semantic relatedness of the articles, and therefore acts as an approximation of humans' semantic world models. However, links between articles are added at the discretion of the article's author. While these links are often strongly semantically related, the amount of links and the degree of relatedness between linked articles can vary wildly. This makes it difficult to derive semantic relations from graph structure alone.

Wikispeedia is a game where the goal is for users to navigate from a source to a target article in a small version of Wikipedia \cite{semdistances}\cite{humanwayfinding}. Data collected from this game offers insight into the semantic relatedness of articles without direct connections, as well as the degree of relatedness (the semantic distance) between articles. West et al. \cite{humanwayfinding} demonstrated that semantic distance between concepts can be inferred from this game data.

In the interest of efficient navigation, it is worthwhile to investigate the relationship between the semantic distances of concepts and navigation speed. Understanding how semantic distance affects the time taken to move from a source to a target article offers opportunities to improve graph organisation. For example, rather than only having links added by authors, Wikipedia could provide additional links to concepts that minimise navigation time. This intuition can also be applied to the organisation of other semantic information networks. 

We propose that navigation in Wikispeedia can be understood by viewing semantic navigation as analogous to physical movement through space. In motor control tasks, Fitts' law \cite{fitts_information_1964} demonstrates a logarithmic relationship between movement time, distance to target, and target width. Wikispeedia navigation strategies tend to follow a two phase structure: initial movement towards a hub article, where large semantic distances are covered quickly, followed by localisation towards the target article where smaller semantic distances are covered over more time. This is similar to the two movement phases observed in Fitts' law tasks: initial fast movement over a large distance towards the target followed by slower more precise movements. This similarity suggests that Wikipedia navigation may follow similar principles to physical movement towards a target and could therefore also be modelled by a log-linear relationship between analogous time, distance, and width metrics. 

We investigated this relationship by identifying several possible conceptual mappings for distance, and width in Wikispeedia games to physical distance, and width respectively. We then calculated indices of difficulty using the distance and width mappings and applied linear regression against game duration. We analysed the resulting correlation coefficients to identify if our chosen conceptual mappings exhibit a log-linear relationship. This leads us to the following research question:

\textbf{RQ: Can we find conceptual mappings for signal and noise in Shannon's theorom that have a log-linear relationship with the number of clicks?}

The paper is structured as follows. In \Cref{sec:background}, we provide background literature that provided us with key insights and motivations. \Cref{sec:materials} the materials we used. In \Cref{sec:methods}, we outline our methodology. In \Cref{sec:results}, we present results with brief descriptions. Finally, \Cref{sec:discussion} discusses our results and provides insights into our findings. In \Cref{sec:future-work} we outline possible future directions and constraints. \Cref{sec:conclusions} contains conclusions. We find that the results suggest that a log-linear relationship exists, but indicate our noise component might not add value.
